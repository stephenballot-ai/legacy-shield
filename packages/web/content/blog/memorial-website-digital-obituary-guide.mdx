## The Phone Calls End. The Silence Begins.

After the funeral, after the flowers have died and the casserole dishes have been returned, the real grief begins. Family members scatter back to their cities. Friends stop texting. And the person you lost — the one who was everywhere in your life — starts to feel further and further away.

A memorial website can't stop that grief. But it can give something remarkable: a permanent place where your loved one's story lives on. A place where your cousin in California can share a memory. Where your mother's childhood friends in Ohio can find each other thirty years later. Where your children can one day show their own children who their grandfather was.

This guide shows you how to create one — starting today.

## What a Memorial Website Actually Is

A memorial website is a private or semi-public webpage dedicated to someone who has died. It typically includes:

- A **digital obituary** — their story in full, not just the newspaper's 150-word version
- **Photo galleries** — the wedding portrait, the camping trip, the Thursday poker face
- **Video tributes** — recorded messages, old home videos converted to digital
- **A guest book** where friends and family leave messages that are never lost
- **Shared memories** — stories that only certain people carry, now preserved forever

Think of it as the digital version of standing around the kitchen table after a funeral, telling stories about the person you all loved. Except this version lasts forever, and anyone who mattered can be part of it.

## Why You Need One (Even If You Think You Don't)

Most people don't create a memorial website because they don't know it's possible, or it feels like too much work during an impossible time.

Here's what happens without one:

**The Facebook tribute post disappears.** Algorithms deprioritize old content. The comment thread where seventy people shared memories becomes harder to find, then impossible. Facebook's "Memorialized Account" feature freezes a profile but doesn't preserve it in a way that's easily shared or accessed by non-friends.

**The photo collections scatter.** Uncle Mike has some photos. Your sister has others. Your mother's friends have the ones from before you were born. Without a central place, they scatter — hard drives fail, phones are upgraded, albums are lost in moves.

**Stories die with the people who hold them.** There's always someone at a memorial service who says, "I have to tell you this story about your mom." Those stories are fragile. A memorial website is where they go.

## How to Create a Memorial Website: Step by Step

### Step 1: Choose a Platform

You have several options:

**Dedicated memorial platforms** like Ever Loved, GatheringUs, or ForeverMissed offer templates specifically designed for this purpose. They typically cost $0–$20/month and handle hosting, photo galleries, and guest books.

**Personal websites** (WordPress, Squarespace) give you more control but require more technical effort. Good option if you want it to look and feel unique.

### Step 2: Gather the Materials

Before you start building, collect:

- At least 10–20 photos spanning their whole life
- Key dates: birthday, wedding, career milestones, children, grandchildren
- Their own words: emails, letters, voicemails, social media posts they'd want preserved
- Contact info for people who knew them — because the guest book is only as good as who knows it exists
- Any video footage, even short clips from a phone

### Step 3: Write the Digital Obituary

The newspaper obituary is written for word-count limits and funeral home templates. The digital obituary has no such restrictions.

Write it in three parts:

**Who they were** — not just their job title and survivors, but who they *actually* were. The teacher who stayed late. The neighbor who always brought food. The stubborn optimist who believed things would work out.

**What they built** — their relationships, their community, their contributions. The people they changed.

**What they'd want us to remember** — this is the hardest part to write, and the most important.

A digital obituary should feel like it was written by someone who loved them. Because it should be.

### Step 4: Invite the People Who Mattered

A memorial website without visitors is just a folder of files. Send personal invitations to:

- Immediate and extended family
- Close friends
- Former colleagues and teachers
- People from different chapters of their life — the childhood neighborhood, the first job, the church community

Include a personal note: *"I created this space to keep [name]'s story alive. I'd love for you to share a memory."*

### Step 5: Secure It for the Long Term

This is where most memorial websites fail.

The hosting bill gets forgotten. The platform shuts down. The domain expires. A few years after creating it with love, the site disappears.

To protect a memorial website long-term:

- **Prepay hosting** for 5–10 years if possible
- **Register the domain** and set it to auto-renew
- **Download backups** of all content annually
- **Designate someone** to maintain the site — or use a service that handles this automatically

## Your Phone Is Your Life

When Amira fled Syria in 2015, she carried almost nothing. No passport. No documents. Just her phone — and on it, photos of her family, screenshots of her marriage certificate, a scanned copy of her university degree, and voice messages from her mother she'd never be able to recreate.

That phone was her entire identity.

For millions of refugees and asylum seekers across Europe, this is not a metaphor. Your digital records are your proof of who you are, where you come from, what happened to you. They are your evidence in asylum hearings. Your connection to family you may never see again. Your way of proving relationships, education, and history to bureaucracies that will not believe you without documentation.

They are also a target.

If the wrong people — border agents from the country you fled, militias, criminal networks, even well-meaning but clumsy institutions — can access what's on your phone or in your cloud storage, they can harm you. Or your family still at home.

The question isn't just how to protect these records from persecution. It's also: if something happens to you, can your family access what they need?

Both problems are real. Both need a plan.

## Why Digital Records Are Uniquely Dangerous for Refugees

For most people in Europe, their phone being seized means lost photos and the inconvenience of changing passwords. For a refugee, a phone seizure can mean:

- **Exposing family members** still living in dangerous regions. Contacts, photos, and messages can be used to identify and threaten people you love.
- **Revealing your location history** to the country you fled, enabling persecution.
- **Undermining your asylum case**, if communications are taken out of context.
- **Exposing your network** of support contacts — journalists, advocates, other refugees — putting multiple people at risk.

Several EU member states have legally authorized police and border authorities to search the phones of asylum seekers. In Germany, the BAMF (Bundesamt für Migration und Flüchtlinge) has had the authority to review phone data to verify identity since 2017. In the Netherlands, IND (Immigratie en Naturalisatiedienst) can request device data. In the UK, phone searches of asylum seekers are routine.

This isn't always malicious — authorities are often trying to verify identity claims. But it means your digital records are not private in the way you might assume.

## The Documents You Must Protect — and Preserve

Here's what you need to balance:

**Protect from unauthorized access:**
- Contact lists that could expose family in dangerous regions
- Messages discussing your plans, route, or network
- Photos that could be geotagged or used to identify others
- Financial details (bank accounts, hawala contacts, crypto wallets)

**Preserve for your future and your family:**
- Identity documents (passport scans, national ID, birth certificate)
- Marriage and family documents (if trying to reunite)
- Educational and professional credentials
- Medical records
- Asylum decision documents and correspondence
- Any evidence you provided in your asylum case

The challenge: the same records that prove who you are can also endanger you.

The solution is **layered access** — some things encrypted and locked, some things accessible to trusted people, none of it all in one place.

## How to Protect Your Digital Records

### 1. Use Encrypted Cloud Storage (But Know Its Limits)

Services like Proton Drive, Tresorit, or encrypted folders in Nextcloud give you cloud storage where even the service provider cannot read your files. This is different from Google Drive or Dropbox, where Google and Dropbox can see your data.

- Store your most sensitive documents (identity, family, asylum evidence) in encrypted cloud storage
- Use a strong, unique password for this account
- Enable two-factor authentication — but use an authenticator app, not SMS (a SIM can be seized)
- Store the recovery codes somewhere physically safe

**Limit**: If you die or are detained, your family will need the password. This is the central tension of digital legacy planning.

### 2. Separate Your "Safe" Records from Your "Sensitive" Records

Don't keep everything in one account. Consider:

- A "public-facing" account (email, social media, photos you want people to see) — this account can be seized without catastrophic consequences
- A "sensitive" account (identity documents, asylum correspondence, family contacts) — encrypted, with very few people who know it exists

If your phone is searched, the officer will see the first account. The second stays protected.

### 3. Use Signal for Sensitive Communications

WhatsApp is encrypted in transit but your messages can still be read by law enforcement with a warrant in most EU countries. Signal offers significantly stronger protections, including disappearing messages.

For communications with family in dangerous regions, consider:
- Signal with disappearing messages (7 days or less)
- Never using real names or specific locations in messages
- Regularly clearing your conversation history after confirming receipt

### 4. The "Emergency Wipe" — Know How to Use It

Every iPhone has a feature where 10 failed passcode attempts wipe the phone. Android has similar options. Know how to activate remote wipe from your cloud account (Find My iPhone or Google Find My Device).

If you are about to have your phone seized and you have time, you may be able to:
- Log out of sensitive accounts on the device
- Trigger remote wipe from a secondary device or trusted person's phone

**Legal caveat**: Refusing to provide a passcode in some jurisdictions is a criminal offense. Know the law in your country. This is about preparation and awareness, not obstruction.

## Who Gets Access When Something Happens to You

Here is the question most people avoid: if you die, are detained, or disappear — who can access your critical documents?

For refugees, this question has life-altering stakes. Your family may need your documents to:
- Prove their relationship to you and claim benefits
- Continue an asylum process on your behalf
- Access money you had saved
- Understand your wishes and carry out your legacy

The answer requires trusting someone — ideally, someone not living in your country of origin.

**Options:**
1. **Give a trusted person your main encrypted storage password** — written down, physically, in a sealed envelope. Tell them where it is.
2. **Appoint a trusted contact in your email** — Gmail's Inactive Account Manager and similar tools allow you to designate someone who gets access after 3, 6, or 12 months of inactivity.
3. **Store a master document** with your trusted person — not passwords (those change), but instructions: "My important documents are in [service], contact [this person] who has the access code."

## What UNHCR and Legal Advocates Say

UNHCR and refugee legal advocates increasingly advise asylum seekers to document their journey and evidence carefully — both for asylum claims and for their own protection. This means:

- Taking timestamped, geolocated photos of key events (with caution about what geolocations reveal)
- Keeping copies of all official correspondence in separate, encrypted storage
- Notifying a trusted third party of the existence and location of key documents

The same organizations note that digital privacy is increasingly fragile at borders. The advice: prepare for the worst while securing the best.

## The Dual Threat — and the Plan

The hardest thing about digital legacy planning for refugees is that you face two simultaneous threats:
1. **Your data is too accessible** (to authorities, persecutors, criminal networks)
2. **Your data is not accessible enough** (to your family, if something happens to you)

Most people in Europe only worry about the second problem. You have to manage both.

This means:
- **Encryption and separation** to protect from unauthorized access
- **Trusted access and documentation** so the right people can help your family when needed

Neither is optional. Both are achievable.

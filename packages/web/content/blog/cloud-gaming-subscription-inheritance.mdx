## Your Game Library Is Worth Thousands — And Your Family Can't Have It

Think about the games you've bought over the years. The PlayStation Store purchases. The Xbox titles you've collected. The Nintendo games tied to your Switch account. The Steam library you've built since 2008.

Now imagine your family finding out — after you're gone — that none of it can be passed on.

This isn't a hypothetical. It's the legal reality for millions of gamers right now. And if you're an expat living across international borders, the problem gets even more complicated.

## The Dirty Secret in Every Terms of Service

Every major gaming platform has the same clause buried in their Terms of Service: your account is **personal and non-transferable**.

Sony's PlayStation Network Terms of Service states that your account — and everything purchased through it — belongs to you personally and cannot be transferred to another person. When you die, so does your access to every game, every DLC, every saved progress file stored in the cloud.

Microsoft's Xbox and Game Pass subscriptions are the same. Nintendo's accounts are perhaps the strictest: Nintendo has explicitly stated that accounts cannot be inherited and recommends that family members contact support, though there is no formal inheritance process in place.

Steam (Valve) has a similar non-transferability policy, though there have been community-driven conversations about this for years with no formal resolution.

You don't *own* your digital game library. You own a **licence to access** it — one that expires the moment you do.

## Why This Hits Expats Differently

If you're an expat — living in the Netherlands, Germany, France, or elsewhere in Europe while maintaining accounts from your home country — this problem multiplies.

Your PlayStation account might be registered in the UK, your Steam in the US, and your Nintendo account in Japan. Each platform operates under different jurisdictions with different Terms of Service interpretations. Inheritance laws in your country of residence may conflict with the platform's policies.

European Union consumer protection laws are evolving on digital goods, but they haven't fully caught up with death and inheritance scenarios. Some EU member states, like Germany, have seen court cases arguing that digital assets should be inheritable — but platform Terms of Service haven't been updated to reflect this.

The practical result: your grieving family is left navigating a patchwork of corporate policies and international legal confusion during one of the worst moments of their lives.

## What Actually Happens to Your Account

Here's the typical journey:

**1. The platform doesn't know you've died.** Unless someone contacts support, your subscriptions keep auto-renewing until the payment method fails or is cancelled. This is money leaving your estate.

**2. When family contacts support**, they face a wall. PlayStation, Xbox, Nintendo, and Steam all have limited — if any — formal processes for deceased account holders. Some platforms may grant temporary "memorial" access. None offer straightforward inheritance of purchased content.

**3. Purchased games are lost.** The digital library you built over decades is gone. Your family may be able to access your console hardware, but the cloud-based licences evaporate.

**4. Subscriptions like PlayStation Plus or Xbox Game Pass**, being recurring subscriptions rather than purchases, are even more clearly non-inheritable. The moment the payment stops, access ends.

## The Multiplayer Legacy Problem

There's an emotional dimension here that often gets overlooked.

Many gamers share their hobby with family. A father who played online games with his adult children. A gamer whose save files contain hundreds of hours of shared memories. A collection of indie games curated over years with personal meaning.

Those save files. Those screenshots. That shared gaming history. In many cases, it's locked inside a platform account that no one else can access.

Some platforms offer "family sharing" features — PlayStation's Share Play, Xbox Family Settings, Nintendo Family Group — but these are designed for living family members, not post-death access. They don't solve the inheritance problem.

## What You Can Do Right Now

The legal landscape may not be on your side, but you're not helpless. Here's what proactive gamers and expats should do today:

**Document everything.** Create a secure record of every gaming account you hold: platform name, email address, username, and rough value of your library. Do NOT store passwords in plaintext — use a proper digital vault solution.

**Communicate your wishes clearly.** Include your gaming accounts in your digital estate plan. Write down which accounts matter most to you and what you'd like to happen to them. Even if the platform won't legally allow transfer, your family needs this information.

**Enable family sharing while you're alive.** If you want your family to benefit from your digital library, set up family sharing *now* — while you're living and can authorize it. On PlayStation, Steam, and Xbox, this means designating a "primary" console or account-sharing arrangement.

**Check for platform-specific options.** Some platforms are slowly introducing legacy contact features (Facebook's Memorialization, Apple's Legacy Contact). Gaming platforms haven't followed suit yet, but this may change. Stay informed.

**Consider the subscription vs. purchase distinction.** Subscriptions (PlayStation Plus, Xbox Game Pass) are definitively non-inheritable. Purchased titles are *slightly* different — there's at least an argument to be made, depending on jurisdiction. Focus your documentation efforts on purchased libraries.

**Talk to a local lawyer.** If you're an expat, this means a lawyer who understands both your country of residence *and* international digital asset law. This is a niche area, but it's growing.

## The Bigger Picture: Digital Legacy Planning

Your gaming accounts are one piece of a much larger puzzle: your complete digital legacy.

Streaming services, cloud storage, social media accounts, cryptocurrency, domain names, email archives — all of these have the same problem. The legal framework hasn't kept pace with the digital economy.

The first step is acknowledging that this matters. The second step is doing something about it — before your family is left scrambling in a grief-filled, bureaucratic nightmare.

Your digital life has value. Treat it that way.

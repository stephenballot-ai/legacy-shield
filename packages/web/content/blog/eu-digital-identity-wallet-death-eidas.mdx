## A New Master Key to Your Life—With a Fatal Flaw

By 2026, millions of Europeans are carrying the EU Digital Identity Wallet (EUDI) on their phones. Under eIDAS 2.0, it's not just a digital ID card—it's a master key to modern life. Banks verify mortgage applications with it. Hospitals pull medical records through it. Government agencies process permits. Your landlord confirms your residency status. Your employer validates your professional credentials.

The EU's ambition is clear: one cryptographically secure digital identity that follows you across all 27 member states. For expats, this has been transformative. No more struggling at a Dutch municipality with paper proof of address. No more explaining your German driving licence to a French notaire.

But there's a question nobody is asking at the official launch events: **what happens to your EUDI wallet when you die?**

## The Answer Is Both Simple and Devastating

Your EU Digital Identity Wallet does not survive your death. When an issuing authority receives notification of death, the wallet—and every verified credential inside it—is revoked. This is by design. Identity fraud targeting deceased individuals is a well-documented problem, and the architects of eIDAS 2.0 built in hard revocation for exactly that reason.

What disappears with your wallet:
- **Verified healthcare records** — your medical history and digital prescriptions
- **Professional qualifications** — university degrees and certified skills
- **Property linkages** — documents connected to what you own
- **Travel documents** — digital passport and driving licence
- **Financial connections** — eIDAS-verified links to your bank, pension, and broker

For your family, this creates an immediate crisis. Probate requires proving what you owned. Your executor needs account access. Your dependents need your insurance records. And the master key to all of it—your EUDI wallet—is gone before the funeral.

## For Expats, the Problem Is Exponentially Worse

If you live in a country other than the one on your passport, your EUDI wallet is often the thread that holds your legal existence there together. Your work permit entitlement, health insurance registration, and banking relationship may all trace back to your eIDAS credentials.

When those credentials are revoked at death, your family may find themselves without even basic information about your legal status in a foreign country. Combine that with language barriers, different inheritance laws in your home and host countries, and you have a tangle that can take months—or years—to unravel.

The EU Succession Regulation (EU 650/2012) helps with cross-border inheritance, but it was written before the EUDI wallet existed. The regulation says nothing about what happens when the digital identity of the deceased is wiped. That gap is yours to fill—proactively—while you still can.

## Five Steps You Can Take This Weekend

**1. Build a Digital Asset Inventory**

Treat your EUDI wallet as an access point and ask: what would stop working the moment this access point disappeared? Every service, account, and document dependent on your EUDI credentials belongs on your list. Write it down. Update it annually.

**2. Store Copies Outside the Wallet**

Your EUDI wallet holds *authoritative* credentials, but the underlying documents exist independently: your passport, degree certificate, insurance policy, property deed. Scan them. Encrypt them. Store them somewhere your family can reach without needing your eIDAS identity to unlock the door.

**3. Appoint a Digital Executor**

Many EU jurisdictions now allow you to name a digital executor in your will—someone specifically tasked with managing your digital estate: notifying platforms, closing accounts, accessing stored documents. If your will predates the EUDI wallet, update it. The one-hour meeting with your notary now is infinitely cheaper than the six-month legal dispute later.

**4. Use Encryption That Doesn't Depend on Your EUDI Identity**

Any storage service that uses your eIDAS login will inherit its fate when you die. A separate, zero-knowledge encrypted vault—opened with a key only your chosen person holds—survives your death because it was never tied to your government-issued identity in the first place.

**5. Write a Plain-Language Letter of Wishes**

Attach a letter to your will that explains, in human terms, what accounts exist, who can access what, and what steps your family should take in the first 30 days. Assume they know nothing about eIDAS, nothing about digital wallets, and that they are grieving while trying to read it.

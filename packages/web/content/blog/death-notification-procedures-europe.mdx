## The Phone Calls Nobody Wants to Make

The moment someone dies, a clock starts ticking. Grief is already overwhelming. And then the paperwork begins.

Banks. Government agencies. Pension offices. Landlords. Insurance companies. Subscription services. Each one needs to be told. Each one has a different process. Each one will ask for documents you may not know how to get.

Most families navigate this alone, making it up as they go. This guide exists so you don't have to.

---

## First 24–48 Hours: Immediate Steps

### Get the Official Death Certificate

Before you can notify anyone, you need proof of death. In most European countries, this involves a doctor or hospital issuing a **medical certificate of death**, which is then taken to the relevant civil registration authority.

**What happens in practice:**

- If the death occurred in a hospital, the hospital handles the medical certificate. Ask them explicitly what their process is.
- If the death was at home, a GP or out-of-hours doctor must attend and issue the certificate.
- If the death was unexpected or unattended, police must be informed before the body can be moved.

Keep at least **10 certified copies** of the death certificate. You will need them for almost every notification, and many institutions will not accept photocopies.

## Week One: Notify the Authorities

### Civil Registration

Every country in Europe requires a death to be officially registered with the civil authorities — this is separate from the medical certificate.

- **UK**: Register with the local register office within 5 days.
- **Netherlands**: Report to the *gemeente* (municipality) where the death occurred within 24 hours. You'll receive the *overlijdensakte* (death deed).
- **Germany**: Report to the *Standesamt* (civil registry office) within 3 working days.
- **France**: Declare the death at the *mairie* (town hall) of the place of death, usually within 24 hours.
- **Italy**: Notify the *Comune* (municipality) within 24 hours.
- **Spain**: File the *declaración de defunción* at the *Registro Civil* within 24 hours.

This registration produces the official death certificate used by all other institutions.

### National Insurance / Tax Authorities

Notify the tax authority early. Outstanding tax obligations don't disappear at death — they become the estate's problem. In most countries, the tax office will also freeze certain accounts or benefits once notified.

- UK: HMRC
- Netherlands: Belastingdienst
- Germany: Finanzamt
- France: Direction Générale des Finances Publiques (DGFiP)
- Italy: Agenzia delle Entrate
- Spain: Agencia Tributaria

## Week Two: Banks and Financial Institutions

Once you have certified death certificates and the civil registration document, approach the banks.

**What you need to bring:**
- Certified copy of the death certificate
- Your own ID
- Proof of your relationship to the deceased (will, or documents showing you are next of kin)
- Depending on the country, a *certificate of inheritance* may be required before any assets can be released

Each bank will freeze the deceased's individual accounts upon notification. Joint accounts are usually more straightforward, but rules vary by country and by bank.

**Important:** Do not delay bank notification. Standing orders and direct debits continue to run until the bank is informed, depleting estate funds.

### Pensions and Benefits

- State pension: notify the national pensions authority
- Occupational pension: notify the employer's pension scheme
- Any benefit payments (disability, unemployment, housing benefit) must also be stopped — overpayments will be reclaimed from the estate

## Week Three and Beyond: The Long Tail

This is the part most guides skip. Here is where families get caught months later when a bill arrives in a dead person's name, or a subscription silently renews.

### Insurances

- Life insurance: notify the insurer with a certified death certificate and claim form
- Health insurance: cancel the policy, claim any outstanding reimbursements
- Home and car insurance: update or transfer policies if the estate is being managed

### Utilities and Services

Contact each provider with proof of death. Most will:
- Transfer the account to another family member or the estate
- Close it and issue a final bill

Keep a list. The average person in Europe has 8–12 recurring service contracts. Even a gym membership or a magazine subscription becomes a nuisance if not cancelled.

### Digital Accounts

This is where things get difficult. Most platforms — email providers, streaming services, social media — were never designed for death. Many require specific processes:

- **Google**: Submit an account closure request with proof of death
- **Apple**: Requires a court order in many countries to access a locked device
- **Facebook/Meta**: Can be memorialized or deleted with proper documentation
- **Banking apps**: Access dies with the account holder; notify banks separately

## The Thing Nobody Tells You

Administrative grief is real. The average estate in Europe takes **6 to 18 months** to fully settle, not weeks. Families describe the paperwork as a second bereavement — just when they thought they could start recovering, another letter arrives.

The families who cope best are the ones whose loved one left organised information behind: a list of accounts, a copy of the will, access credentials, insurance policy numbers, and a note explaining where to find everything.

That is not morbid. That is a profound act of love.

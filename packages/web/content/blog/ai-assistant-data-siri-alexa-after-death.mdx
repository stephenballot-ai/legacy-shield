## They Know More About You Than Your Doctor Does

Every morning you ask Alexa for the weather. Every evening you tell Google Assistant to set a reminder. Every commute you ask Siri to call your partner, play your playlist, navigate to the office.

Your AI assistants have been listening — and remembering — for years.

They know what time you wake up, what you worry about before bed, which medications you take, which political podcasts you listen to, what fights you've had with family members, and which searches you made at 2am that you'd never admit to anyone.

And when you die, all of that data is still sitting in a server somewhere — owned not by your family, but by Apple, Amazon, or Google.

## What Your Voice Assistants Actually Collect

The data these companies store goes far beyond your grocery lists:

**Amazon Alexa** stores every voice interaction you've ever had — unless you manually delete them. That includes voice recordings, transcriptions, your smart home configuration, shopping history, music preferences, and your daily routine patterns.

**Google Assistant** maintains a complete searchable history of every query you've made, synced across all your devices. It knows your location history, your calendar, your contacts, and every command you've ever spoken.

**Apple Siri** stores queries on Apple's servers, linked to your Apple ID. Your Siri Shortcuts, routines, and home automations paint a detailed picture of how you live.

That data is not just metadata. It is a portrait of a person's inner life.

## The Problem: None of This Was Built for Death

Here's the uncomfortable reality: AI assistants were designed as personal tools. They were never designed to be inherited.

When you die, your Alexa voice history doesn't pass to your family like a photo album. Your Google Assistant routines don't transfer to your partner like a car key. The companies that built these products have no clear, humane process for what happens next.

What actually happens depends on the company:

- **Amazon**: Your account remains active until someone contacts Amazon to close it. Voice recordings remain on their servers. Your family may be able to access it if they have your password — but Amazon may not recognize their legal right to do so.
- **Google**: Google has an "Inactive Account Manager" that you can configure in advance. Without it, your family must go through a legal process to request data access — which may take months.
- **Apple**: Apple will not provide account access to family members without a court order. Even with one, the process is slow and uncertain.

Meanwhile, years of your most intimate data just sits there — or gets deleted after inactivity — with no family notification, no legal clarity.

## The Expat Dimension

If you live in Europe, this situation is even more complicated.

Under the **GDPR**, the rights granted to individuals — including the right to access and delete data — generally do not automatically extend to family members after death. Member states handle this differently, and no EU-wide standard exists for posthumous data rights.

As an expat, you may have accounts linked to your home country, your country of residence, and multiple languages. Your digital estate crosses borders in ways that your physical estate doesn't.

Your family — potentially living in a different country — faces a bureaucratic maze: foreign company terms of service, GDPR data request procedures in a language they may not speak, and no clear legal entitlement to the data of someone they loved.

## The Conversation Nobody Is Having

Most estate planning focuses on property, bank accounts, and physical possessions. It almost never addresses AI assistant data — and that's a serious gap.

Ask yourself:

- Does your partner know how to access your Alexa account to cancel it or download your data?
- Have you set up Google's Inactive Account Manager to share your data with a trusted contact?
- If your Siri shortcuts contain personal health routines, who should know about them?
- Is there data in your voice history that you'd want deleted immediately — data that could cause pain or embarrassment to your family?

These are not hypothetical questions. They're becoming real ones faster than anyone expected.

## What You Should Do Today

You don't need to solve the entire digital estate problem right now. But there are immediate, concrete steps you can take:

1. **Review your Alexa voice history** at alexa.amazon.com. Delete anything you wouldn't want your family to find. Set automatic deletion for future recordings.

2. **Configure Google's Inactive Account Manager** at myaccount.google.com. Choose a trusted contact who can receive your data or request deletion.

3. **Audit your Siri and HomeKit setup.** Write down which automations and shortcuts matter to your household, so your partner can reproduce them or disable them.

4. **Document your AI assistant accounts** — login credentials, account emails, device lists — in a secure, accessible place that your executor can find.

5. **Think about what you want deleted immediately** upon death. Voice recordings of conversations with your children? Medical queries? Personal diary-style reminders? That list belongs in your digital end-of-life plan.

## The Window Is Now

The companies that build these products are slowly, quietly building better posthumous tools. But they are far behind the reality of how deeply embedded AI assistants are in daily life.

The gap between what exists legally and what your family will need practically is significant — and it's your responsibility to bridge it.

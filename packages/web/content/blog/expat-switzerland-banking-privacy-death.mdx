## The Call No Family Expects

Your sister calls you from Amsterdam. Your mother — who moved to Zürich thirty years ago for a job that turned into a life — has died. After the grief comes the paperwork. The apartment. The car. The pension.

And the Swiss bank accounts.

She calls UBS. The representative is polite, professional, and entirely unhelpful. Does her mother have an account there? They cannot confirm. Are there funds? They cannot say. Is she the legal heir? That may well be, but they need documentation. What documentation? A Swiss inheritance certificate. Where does she get one? From the civil authorities in the canton where her mother lived. How long does that take? Several weeks, at minimum.

She hangs up and stares at the wall. This is Switzerland — the most organized country in the world. And she has no idea what her mother left behind.

## Why Swiss Banking Secrecy Outlives Its Owner

Switzerland's *Bankgeheimnis* — banking secrecy — has been a legal pillar since 1934. While international pressure has dramatically reduced its scope in recent decades (the Automatic Exchange of Information largely ended the era of hidden offshore wealth), it still shapes how Swiss banks interact with third parties.

The problem for heirs is subtle but serious: Swiss banks treat *potential heirs* as third parties until they can prove otherwise. Until you produce the correct documentation, a Swiss bank will not confirm whether an account exists, let alone reveal its balance.

This is not obstruction. It is procedure. But procedure, when you are grieving and racing against deadlines, feels like a wall.

## What Swiss Law Actually Requires

Switzerland's inheritance system is governed by the *Zivilgesetzbuch* (Civil Code, ZGB). Here is what happens after a death:

**1. The Local Authority Opens the Estate**
When a Swiss resident dies, the civil registrar in their canton notifies the relevant authorities. An inventory of the estate begins — but this is a formal legal process, not an automatic notification to banks.

**2. Heirs Need an Erbschein or Erbbescheinigung**
To access bank accounts, heirs must produce an *Erbschein* (full certificate of inheritance) or an *Erbbescheinigung* (simplified version). This document proves your right to act on behalf of the estate.

Obtaining it requires:
- The death certificate
- Proof of your relationship to the deceased
- Documentation of any will
- In many cases, an in-person visit to the cantonal authority

For heirs living abroad — children in the UK, a spouse in Canada — this means international coordination, sworn translations, apostilles, and often a local Swiss lawyer or notary.

**3. The Process Takes Weeks, Sometimes Months**
Swiss administrative processes are thorough. They are also slow. For heirs with documentation in other languages, the timeline stretches further. While you wait, accounts are frozen.

## The Dormant Account Problem

Here is a fact that surprises most people: Switzerland has a documented history of unclaimed accounts worth billions of francs.

After World War II, thousands of accounts held assets of victims whose families had been killed or scattered. A 1995 law now requires Swiss banks to actively search for heirs when an account becomes dormant for ten years. If no heir appears within fifty years, the funds transfer to the relevant canton.

Modern versions of this problem happen every day. An expat dies. Their family in another country doesn't know about the Swiss brokerage account — opened quietly fifteen years ago, in a name they don't recognize, holding assets the deceased never mentioned. The account quietly goes dormant. No one claims it.

This is not a rare tragedy. It is a predictable outcome of poor planning.

## Digital Banking Makes It Worse

Your Swiss accounts don't just exist in a branch. They live in an app on your phone, behind a password you never shared, protected by two-factor authentication tied to a SIM card that gets disconnected when you die.

Your family cannot log in. They cannot see statements. They cannot know whether your portfolio holds CHF 5,000 or CHF 500,000.

The bank will not help them without the *Erbschein*. And getting the *Erbschein* requires proving the estate — which, circularly, requires knowing the estate exists.

This loop trips up even organized families.

## For Expats: An Extra Layer of Complexity

If you are a non-Swiss national living in Switzerland, your estate carries additional complications that most people discover too late.

**Which country's law applies?** Switzerland is not in the EU, so the EU Succession Regulation (Brussels IV) does not automatically apply. Switzerland has bilateral inheritance treaties with some countries (including Germany and the US), but the interaction between Swiss and foreign law is genuinely complex — and often depends on where the deceased was domiciled at the time of death.

**Where should a will be filed?** A Swiss holographic will — entirely handwritten, dated, and signed in your own hand — is valid under Swiss law. But your home country may not recognize it automatically. A notarized will submitted to the Swiss civil registry creates a clearer paper trail.

**Who manages the estate?** Swiss law allows appointment of a *Willensvollstrecker* (estate executor). Without one, and with heirs scattered across three countries, coordinating the estate can become an expensive legal exercise in itself.

**What about forced heirship?** Swiss *Pflichtteilsrecht* guarantees minimum inheritance shares to spouses and descendants — even if your will attempts to limit them. If your estate plan was designed around your home country's rules, it may conflict with Swiss requirements.

## Three Steps That Change Everything

You do not need to solve all of this today. But these three actions create the foundation your family will need.

**1. Create a list of your Swiss financial accounts.** Every bank account, brokerage, pension fund (the *Pensionskasse* second pillar and *Säule 3a* third pillar), and life insurance policy. Store this list somewhere your family can actually find it — not inside the Swiss bank's secure messaging portal, which they will not be able to access without your login.

**2. Understand what your heirs will actually need.** Talk to a Swiss *Notar* (notary) or inheritance lawyer now, not later. Ask which documents you can prepare today: a registered will, a notarized contact list, an executor appointment. Preparation costs a fraction of the legal fees chaos generates.

**3. Do not assume your home-country estate plan covers Switzerland.** A will valid in the Netherlands, Germany, or France may not be sufficient for your Swiss assets without additional steps. If you hold assets in multiple countries, you need coordinated legal advice across all of them.

## Your Family Deserves a Path, Not a Wall

Swiss banking privacy served real purposes. It protected legitimate assets. In some ways, it still does. But when you are gone, that privacy does not protect you — it becomes a barrier your grieving family must climb, with legal documents in unfamiliar languages, sworn translations, and months of waiting.

Your Swiss accounts could be one of the most valuable things you leave behind. Or one of the most inaccessible. The difference is whether you planned for it.

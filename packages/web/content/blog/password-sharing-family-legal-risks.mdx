## The Password You Shared Today Could Become a Legal Problem Tomorrow

Your daughter knows your Netflix password. Your husband has your Amazon login. Your brother can get into your Gmail "just in case." It feels responsible — practical, even. You're making sure someone can help you if something goes wrong.

But here's what nobody tells you: the moment you die, those shared passwords shift from a helpful arrangement into a legal gray zone. And in some cases, your family members could face real legal consequences for using credentials you voluntarily gave them.

This isn't about streaming passwords and whose account can be on how many screens. This is about what happens when the account holder is gone — and someone who loves them is still logged in.

## What the Law Actually Says

Most online services operate under Terms of Service agreements that prohibit account sharing or mandate that credentials are personal and non-transferable. In the UK, the Computer Misuse Act 1990 criminalizes unauthorized access to computer systems. In the US, the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (CFAA) takes a similar stance. In the EU, Directive 2013/40/EU covers unauthorized access to information systems.

The critical word is *unauthorized*. When you're alive, you can authorize someone to use your account. But the moment you die, that authorization may expire — legally speaking, the account holder no longer exists to grant permission.

What does this mean in practice? If your spouse continues using your Netflix account after your death, they may technically be violating the platform's terms. That's unlikely to result in criminal charges. But your online banking? Your investment accounts? Your business email with client data? The legal stakes are considerably higher.

## The Real Risk: Financial and Business Accounts

Nobody is going to prosecute your grieving widow for watching your Netflix queue. But consider these scenarios:

**Online banking access.** In most countries, it is illegal to access a deceased person's bank account — even if you know the password — without proper legal authority such as Letters of Administration or Probate. Using a shared password to access your partner's account after their death is technically unauthorized access, regardless of your relationship or intentions.

**Investment accounts.** Brokerage firms take unauthorized access seriously. If a family member uses a deceased person's credentials to monitor or move investments, they could face civil liability, asset freezes, or worse.

**Business accounts.** If you're self-employed or run a business, your professional email, accounting software, or client management system contains data that belongs to clients, not just to you. A family member accessing these systems after your death — even trying to help wind down the business — could violate confidentiality agreements or data protection regulations like GDPR.

**Health portals and insurance.** Medical records and insurance portals contain highly sensitive data. Unauthorized access, even by next-of-kin, can have legal consequences in jurisdictions with strict health data protection laws.

## What Happens When Digital Access Becomes a Legal Battle

Here's a scenario that plays out more often than you'd think.

A husband dies suddenly. His wife knows his bank login — he shared it years ago so she could check the joint account. But the current account was in his name only. She logs in. She moves money to cover immediate expenses — the funeral, the mortgage. Legally, she's just committed unauthorized access and potentially theft, even though she's his wife and the money will eventually be hers through probate.

Or consider a small business owner who dies. His sister, trying to help, accesses his business email to notify clients and cancel contracts. She's accessing a system that contains client data protected by GDPR. She's now potentially liable under data protection law, even with the best intentions.

These situations don't necessarily end in prosecution. But they can complicate probate, trigger investigations, freeze assets, and create enormous additional stress for families already in grief.

## The Right Way to Give Family Access After You're Gone

The solution isn't to refuse to share passwords. It's to have a **legal access framework** that governs what happens to your digital accounts after death.

**Create a digital legacy document.** This is a document — stored securely, not emailed or left on a sticky note — that lists your accounts, credentials, and crucially, your explicit instructions for each. Your solicitor or notary can include this in your estate planning, giving your designees legal cover to act on your behalf.

**Assign a digital executor.** In your will, name someone specifically responsible for managing your digital estate. Give them the legal authority — not just the passwords — to act on your behalf after your death.

## The Phone Call Nobody Prepares For

It was a Tuesday morning when Maria got the call. Her husband had died suddenly of a heart attack. Within hours, she was fielding condolence messages from family across three countries. By Thursday, she had a more pressing problem: their joint bank account was frozen.

Maria and her husband had lived in the Netherlands for eleven years. They had one joint account for household expenses, their life savings split across two platforms, and a pension fund in his name. She had no idea — none — that the joint account she'd used for groceries the day before would be inaccessible to her within 48 hours of his death.

This is not an unusual story. It's an almost universal one for expats across Europe who haven't thought carefully about what joint account survivorship actually means — legally, practically, and country by country.

## Why "Joint Account" Doesn't Mean What You Think

Most people assume a joint bank account means exactly what it sounds like: both partners have full, equal, ongoing access. That's true while both partners are alive. The moment one partner dies, however, the rules shift — and they shift differently depending on which country's banking system you're using.

There is no single EU-wide rule governing what happens to a joint account after death. Instead, you get a patchwork of national laws, bank policies, and inheritance rules that interact in ways even lawyers find complicated.

## The Netherlands: Gemeenschappelijke Rekening

In the Netherlands, a joint bank account (*gemeenschappelijke rekening*) is typically structured as a *and/or* account — both partners can act independently. This sounds like it provides protection for the surviving partner. In practice, Dutch banks routinely freeze joint accounts upon notification of a death, regardless of the account structure.

Why? Because Dutch law requires banks to ensure the estate is properly administered before funds are distributed. Until a *Verklaring van Erfrecht* (certificate of inheritance) has been issued by a notaris confirming who the legal heirs are, the bank treats the account as part of the estate.

Getting a Verklaring van Erfrecht takes weeks. Sometimes months. The surviving partner may have full legal rights to the funds — but they can't access them while the paperwork is processed. If your partner was the primary earner, this creates an immediate financial emergency.

**What helps:** Having your own personal account with enough to cover 2-3 months of expenses. Keeping a separate emergency fund you control independently.

## Germany: Gemeinschaftskonto and Erbrecht

Germany has two main forms of joint account: *Oder-Konto* (either/or — each partner can act independently) and *Und-Konto* (and — both signatures required). Most couples use the Oder-Konto.

Under German *Erbrecht* (inheritance law), the surviving partner generally retains access to an Oder-Konto immediately — the bank doesn't automatically freeze it. However, the deceased partner's share of the account becomes part of the *Nachlass* (estate), which means the heirs (including children from previous relationships) may have legal claims on it.

The practical danger: if you inherit under German intestacy rules and you're not the sole heir, you may be entitled to the funds but unable to access them cleanly without a *Erbschein* (certificate of inheritance). And if you withdraw funds before the estate is settled, you may face claims from co-heirs.

German banks increasingly require a death certificate and Erbschein before allowing any transactions — even on Oder-Kontos. The process takes 6-12 weeks minimum.

**What helps:** A clear, up-to-date German will (*Testament*) designating your partner as sole heir. Notarized couple's agreement if relevant.

## France: Le Compte Joint

France actually has one of the more survivor-friendly systems in Europe. A French *compte joint* (joint account) is treated as jointly owned property, and the surviving partner retains full access without needing to wait for the estate process.

However, this only applies to funds in the joint account at the time of death. Any assets held separately — which includes most significant savings and investment accounts in France — follow French succession law, which can be complex for international families.

France also has a *réserve héréditaire* — a mandatory share of the estate that must go to children. If your deceased partner had children from a previous relationship, those children have legal claims that cannot be overridden by a will. For expat couples with blended families, this creates serious planning challenges.

**What helps:** Understanding whether you're subject to French succession law or the law of another EU country (EU Succession Regulation 650/2012 lets you choose the law of your nationality in some cases). Consulting a French notaire.

## Italy: Conto Cointestato

Italian joint accounts (*conto cointestato*) are typically split equally — 50/50 — for inheritance purposes. The surviving partner retains access to their 50%, but the deceased partner's 50% enters the estate and is subject to Italian *successione* (inheritance) rules.

Italy has mandatory inheritance shares (*legittima*) similar to France, protecting children and certain relatives regardless of the will. Italian banks are generally quicker to freeze accounts than their French counterparts, requiring documentation before releasing even the surviving partner's half.

The Italian bureaucracy for estate administration — the *dichiarazione di successione* — must be filed within 12 months of death and involves notarized documentation, property valuations, and tax declarations. For expats without fluent Italian or a local notaio, this process can stretch well beyond a year.

**What helps:** A local notaio relationship established before you need one. Italian-language documents properly notarized and stored somewhere accessible.

## Spain: La Cuenta Conjunta

Spain follows a civil law system with significant regional variation. In Catalonia, the Basque Country, and other regions with *fuero* (regional law), inheritance rules differ from the national code.

Spanish joint accounts (*cuenta conjunta*) are treated similarly to Italian ones: the deceased partner's share enters the estate. The *herencia* (inheritance) process requires the estate to be formally accepted or renounced by heirs, and a *declaración de herederos* (declaration of heirs) must be issued by a *notario*.

Spanish banks routinely freeze all accounts associated with a deceased individual — including joint accounts — until the estate is formally settled. This process takes a minimum of 3-6 months, often much longer.

Spain also has EU regulation complexity: if you're a foreign national living in Spain, choosing which country's law governs your estate matters enormously.

**What helps:** A Spanish will (*testamento*) drafted with a notario. Understanding which region's law applies to your assets.

## The One Thing Every Expat Couple Must Do Today

Regardless of which country you live in, the pattern is clear: joint accounts provide less protection than people expect, and the gap between expectation and reality is largest precisely when you're most vulnerable — in the immediate aftermath of losing a partner.

The minimum action every expat couple should take:

1. **Each partner should have their own personal account** with 2-3 months of expenses available independently
2. **Know your country's specific rules** — and check if they've changed (they do)
3. **Have a current will** that is valid in the country where your assets are held
4. **Store critical account information securely** — where it can be found, by someone you trust, when they need it most

That last point is where most couples fail. The information exists — account numbers, bank contacts, pension fund details — but it lives in email threads, old paperwork, memories. When someone dies suddenly, the surviving partner often doesn't know where to start.

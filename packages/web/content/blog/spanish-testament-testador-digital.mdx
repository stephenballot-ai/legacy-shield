## You Did the Responsible Thing — and It's Still Not Enough

You sat down with a Spanish notario. You paid the fees. You signed your testamento and walked out with the quiet confidence of someone who has their affairs in order.

But here's the question no one asked you at that appointment: *What happens to your Gmail account? Your Dropbox full of family photos? Your crypto wallet? Your PayPal balance? Your Spotify account with 15 years of playlists?*

For most people who've written a Spanish testament, the honest answer is: *nothing good*.

## The Gap in Every Spanish Testament

Spanish succession law — governed primarily by the *Código Civil* and regional laws like those in Catalonia, the Basque Country, or Navarra — was written long before anyone had a digital footprint. The law recognizes tangible assets: property, bank accounts, vehicles, physical valuables. Even today, most notarios only ask about what they can list in traditional terms.

Digital assets occupy a legal grey zone. They're not treated uniformly as inheritable property under Spanish law. Your family may have *moral* access to your memories and files, but accessing your accounts without proper documentation is a legal and practical nightmare — and in some cases, technically illegal under data protection frameworks.

The result? Families spend months — sometimes years — locked out of accounts containing irreplaceable content, unrealized financial value, or critical documents.

## What "Digital Assets" Actually Means

When we say digital assets, we mean:

- **Financial**: Cryptocurrency wallets (Bitcoin, Ethereum), PayPal balances, Revolut accounts, stock portfolios on platforms like eToro or Degiro, unclaimed cashback or loyalty points
- **Creative & sentimental**: iCloud or Google Photos libraries, years of WhatsApp messages, personal diaries, writing projects, music collections
- **Professional**: Domains, websites, client databases, SaaS subscriptions with ongoing revenue
- **Identity**: Email accounts — which are the master key to resetting everything else

If you have assets on any of these platforms and no plan, you have a problem. And in Spain, that problem is compounded by bureaucracy.

## The Expat Dimension

If you're an expat living in Spain, you have an additional layer of complexity. Under **EU Succession Regulation 650/2012** (Brussels IV), your estate is governed by the law of the country where you were *habitually resident* at death — meaning Spanish law applies by default, even to your accounts hosted abroad.

You can elect to have the law of your nationality apply instead — but only if you explicitly state this in your will. Many expats don't. And even those who do still face the practical challenge of proving account ownership across multiple jurisdictions to multiple platforms, each with its own death verification process.

An English-speaking expat in Costa del Sol might have assets in the UK, Netherlands, and Germany, a Spanish bank account, crypto on a Maltese exchange, and a Google account governed by Irish jurisdiction. When they die, their Spanish heirs face a labyrinth.

## What a Notario Can (and Cannot) Do

A Spanish notario can draft a legally watertight testament that distributes your property according to Spanish law. They can appoint an executor (*albacea*). They can minimize inheritance tax exposure — and in Spain, *Impuesto sobre Sucesiones y Donaciones* can be significant, though rates vary dramatically by autonomous community.

What a notario cannot do:

- Force platforms like Apple, Google, or Meta to hand over account access
- Provide your heirs with passwords or recovery codes
- Ensure your executor even knows what digital accounts exist

That knowledge lives only with you. And if you don't organize it somewhere accessible, it dies with you.

## Three Steps You Can Take This Week

**1. Inventory your digital life.** Write down every account that has financial value, sentimental value, or access you'd want transferred. Don't underestimate what this list looks like — most people have 100+ accounts.

**2. Decide what should transfer and what should close.** Some things (like crypto or PayPal balances) should transfer to heirs. Others (personal email, social media) might better be memorialized or deleted. Make this decision explicitly.

**3. Store this information securely — and make it accessible.** A document on your laptop that no one can find is useless. A handwritten note in a safe deposit box that your family doesn't know about is also useless. You need secure, accessible storage with clear instructions.

## The Document Problem in Spain

Here's the practical reality: after a death in Spain, your family will need a *certificado de defunción*, often a *testamento* registered with the *Registro Central de Últimas Voluntades*, and potentially a *declaración de herederos*. They'll be dealing with notarios, the Spanish tax authority (*Agencia Tributaria*), banks, and possibly courts — likely while managing grief, distance, and a language barrier.

The last thing they need is to also be guessing what accounts you had, scrambling for passwords, or fighting with customer support teams in multiple countries.

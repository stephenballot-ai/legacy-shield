## The Question Nobody Is Asking

Your 10-year-old is a content creator. They have 187,000 YouTube subscribers, a monetized channel, and they earn €2,800 per month in ad revenue. You manage everything—you set up the Google account, you handle the AdSense payments, you file the taxes. The world sees your child as the star. Legally, you are the operator.

Now imagine you die tomorrow.

What happens to the channel? What happens to the revenue? What happens to your child's digital livelihood—built over years, now sitting inside a Google account with a dead person's name on it?

This is not a hypothetical. The "child creator" economy is enormous. Hundreds of thousands of families across Europe and North America have children earning real income from YouTube. Almost none of them have a plan for what happens when the parent-manager dies.

## Who Actually "Owns" the Channel

Here is the legal reality that most families don't understand: **your child does not own their YouTube channel**.

YouTube's Terms of Service require users to be at least 13 years old. Children under 13 cannot create their own accounts under any circumstances. This means that every "kid creator" channel is legally owned and operated by an adult—almost always a parent.

That parent:
- Created the Google account
- Agreed to YouTube's Terms of Service on their own behalf
- Is the registered AdSense account holder
- Receives all payments
- Is legally responsible for all content

When that parent dies, the channel doesn't automatically pass to the child. It sits inside a deceased person's Google account, locked behind credentials that surviving family members may not have, tied to an AdSense account that will eventually freeze when payment information expires.

## What YouTube's Terms Actually Say

YouTube is explicit: **accounts cannot be transferred**. You cannot sell a YouTube channel. You cannot legally give it to someone else. You cannot leave it in your will in the traditional sense.

This matters enormously for child creator families. Even if you draft a perfect will naming your channel's "successor," YouTube will not honor it. The platform does not recognize inheritance. What they do have is an account access request process for deceased users through Google's Inactive Account Manager—but this process was designed for personal accounts, not monetized business operations. It is slow, cumbersome, and provides read-only access at best.

The AdSense revenue stops flowing the moment the account stops functioning—whether due to expired payment methods, Google freezing the account after detecting unusual activity, or a family member reporting the account holder's death.

## The Guardian Problem

Here is where it gets truly complicated.

When both parents die (or the single managing parent dies), a court must appoint a legal guardian for the minor child. In most jurisdictions, this guardian steps into the parent's shoes for all legal matters—including managing the child's financial interests.

But YouTube and Google are not courts. They don't recognize guardianship orders automatically. A newly appointed guardian wanting to access a deceased parent's Google account to preserve a child's channel must navigate:

1. **Google's bereavement process**: A multi-week process that may grant access to account data, but not the ability to operate the account
2. **Identity verification**: Google requires documentation that can be difficult for guardians to produce quickly
3. **AdSense account complications**: AdSense is a separate legal entity from YouTube. The account may be frozen or transferred, but only after an extensive business review process
4. **Tax obligations**: The estate owes income tax on any revenue earned before death. The pending AdSense balance at time of death becomes part of the taxable estate

Meanwhile, the channel sits dark. Sponsorship deals evaporate. Brand partners move on. Subscribers leave. An audience built over years can collapse in weeks.

## The Revenue Already Earned

There is one element that families consistently overlook: **the money already in the AdSense account at time of death**.

YouTube pays out monthly, with a 30-day lag. At any moment, there is typically 30-60 days of accrued revenue sitting in an AdSense account. For a channel earning €3,000/month, that is €6,000-9,000 that becomes part of the estate.

To claim it, a family member or executor must:
- Prove they have legal authority over the account (executor status, probate documents)
- Navigate Google's account bereavement process
- Wait—often months—for Google to process the request
- Potentially file a separate claim to transfer the AdSense balance

Without planning, this money can simply disappear—Google may eventually close the account and write off the balance.

## What Actually Happens in Practice

A parent dies. The family is in shock. The YouTube channel and its revenue are the last thing anyone is thinking about.

Then, six weeks later:
- The AdSense account stops processing payments (expired card, account flagged)
- The channel continues uploading if auto-scheduling was set up—or it goes completely dark
- Brand sponsorships send emails that nobody answers
- YouTube flags the account for unusual inactivity
- The child's income disappears

By the time anyone thinks to act, the channel may have lost half its subscribers. The audience is gone. Years of work are functionally dead—not because the content disappeared, but because the human system that operated it collapsed.

## How to Plan for This Now

The situation sounds hopeless. It isn't. Here is what you can do today:

### Document Everything
Create a secure document (physical or encrypted digital) that contains:
- The Google account email and password used for the channel
- The recovery email and phone number for the account
- Your AdSense login credentials and bank account details
- All brand partnership contacts and their contract terms
- Your channel's upload schedule and any pre-scheduled content
- Your channel manager's or editor's contact information if you work with anyone

This document goes in your legacy plan, accessible to your executor.

### Designate a Successor Manager
Write into your will that a named individual is to assume management of your child's YouTube channel. While YouTube won't legally honor this, it gives your executor clear authority to act and a documented intention for any court proceedings.

### Consider a Family LLC or Company Structure
In some jurisdictions, moving the channel under a legal entity (rather than a personal Google account) provides continuity after death. The company survives even when its owner changes. This requires legal advice specific to your country, but for high-earning channels it is worth the cost.

### Set Up Google's Inactive Account Manager
Google has a tool that allows you to designate what happens to your account after a period of inactivity or death. You can grant specific trusted contacts access to download account data. This is not a full solution, but it ensures someone can access your files, emails, and account data immediately—not after a six-month legal process.

### Have a Channel Continuity Plan
Who will post for your child if you're gone? Who will communicate with sponsors? Write it down. Have a conversation with the person you're naming. Give them access to your channel management tools now, not later.

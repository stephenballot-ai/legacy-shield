## Your Digital Life Doesn't Stand Still

Last September you updated your digital estate plan. You felt good about it. Thorough. You covered everything.

Then in November you signed up for a new crypto exchange. In January your company changed your email. In March you opened a Revolut account for your Spain trip. In June you finally started that Substack newsletter — 800 subscribers now. In August your partner added you as joint owner of the vacation rental booking account.

Your "complete" digital estate plan is now nine months out of date. And if something happened to you today, your family would be working from a map of a city that no longer exists.

This is the reality of digital estates. They change constantly. An annual inventory isn't a chore — it's the foundation that makes everything else work.

## Why Annual Matters More Than You Think

Most people update their will when they buy a house or have a child. But your digital assets change far more frequently than your physical ones. Consider:

- **The average person adds 10-20 new accounts per year** — streaming services, apps, newsletters, marketplaces
- **Crypto portfolios can change dramatically** in value and composition over 12 months
- **Business tools multiply** as remote work and freelancing grow
- **Subscriptions accumulate silently** — you forget them, but they hold your data

A digital estate plan from three years ago might as well be written in a foreign language. The accounts are different. The passwords are different. The platforms have changed their policies. Some services you relied on no longer exist; others you've never heard of now hold your most sensitive data.

The annual review catches all of this — before it becomes your family's problem.

## The Complete Annual Digital Asset Checklist

Set aside 90 minutes. Get a coffee. Work through this systematically.

### Section 1: Financial Accounts

Go through every financial institution you touched in the last 12 months:

- [ ] Bank accounts — primary, savings, joint accounts
- [ ] Investment accounts — stocks, bonds, funds
- [ ] Crypto wallets — hot wallets, cold storage, exchange accounts
- [ ] PayPal, Wise, Revolut, and other payment platforms
- [ ] Pension accounts and workplace retirement plans
- [ ] Loan and mortgage portals
- [ ] Tax accounts — filing platforms, HMRC gateway, government portals

For each: confirm login details are in your password manager, note the approximate value, and confirm whether you have a beneficiary designated.

**The question to ask yourself**: If I died today, could my executor find this account within a week?

### Section 2: Professional and Business Assets

- [ ] Domain names — all registrars (GoDaddy, Namecheap, Cloudflare)
- [ ] Hosting accounts — VPS servers, cloud hosting, website platforms
- [ ] Professional email accounts — particularly those not on your main provider
- [ ] Freelance platforms — Upwork, Fiverr, Toptal, client portals
- [ ] Business bank accounts and payment processors (Stripe, PayPal Business)
- [ ] LinkedIn and professional social accounts
- [ ] Substack, newsletter platforms, Patreon, and income-generating content
- [ ] Software licenses tied to your identity — development tools, design software

If any of these generate income, your family needs immediate access. Income stops when accounts go dark.

### Section 3: Creative and Intellectual Property

- [ ] Cloud storage — Google Drive, Dropbox, iCloud, OneDrive
- [ ] Photo libraries — Google Photos, iCloud Photos, Lightroom catalog
- [ ] Creative work — Figma files, Adobe Creative Cloud, Canva
- [ ] Written work — Notion, Obsidian, drafts, manuscripts
- [ ] Music — Spotify library, SoundCloud uploads, Bandcamp
- [ ] Video — YouTube channel, Vimeo, editing project files
- [ ] Code repositories — GitHub, GitLab, private repos

This is the category people forget most. Creative work has both sentimental and monetary value. A design portfolio, a photo archive, a repository of code — these don't disappear when you do, but they become inaccessible without your credentials.

### Section 4: Personal and Social

- [ ] Email accounts — primary, secondary, old ones you still receive mail to
- [ ] Social media — Facebook, Instagram, X, TikTok, Reddit, LinkedIn
- [ ] Messaging apps — WhatsApp, Telegram, Signal, iMessage
- [ ] Dating profiles (yes, these need handling too)
- [ ] Gaming accounts — Steam, PlayStation Network, Xbox, Nintendo
- [ ] Subscription services — Netflix, Spotify, Adobe, news subscriptions

For social accounts specifically: decide now whether you want them memorialized, deleted, or transferred. Most platforms have specific policies, and doing this in advance spares your family an enormous amount of friction.

### Section 5: Security Infrastructure

- [ ] Password manager — have you shared emergency access?
- [ ] Two-factor authentication devices — what happens to your phone or security key?
- [ ] Recovery codes — where are they stored?
- [ ] Email accounts used for password recovery — who has access?
- [ ] Biometric data — who knows your phone PIN or laptop password?

This section is the keystone. Without access to your password manager or recovery email, every other account on this list becomes a locked room with no key.

## What to Do After the Inventory

The inventory reveals gaps. Now close them.

**Update your emergency access document.** This is separate from your legal will — it's a practical guide for your executor. Include account names, login methods, and any special notes (like "crypto is on the hardware wallet in the desk drawer").

**Assign beneficiaries where you can.** Some platforms — particularly financial ones — allow you to designate a beneficiary directly. Use this.

**Review your legacy contacts.** Apple, Google, and Facebook all allow you to designate someone who can access your account after death. If you set this up two years ago, confirm the person is still the right choice.

**Flag anything that generates income.** Your executor needs to know within days — not weeks — if you had active subscriptions, rental income through digital platforms, or freelance clients who might still transfer payments.

**Set a reminder for next year.** First week of September. 90 minutes. Same checklist.

## The Cost of Skipping This

A 36-year-old dies suddenly. His wife knows he had crypto but has no idea which exchange or how much. She spends four months and €12,000 in lawyer fees trying to identify and recover assets. She gets back about half.

A freelance photographer dies. Her family finds thousands of photos on various cloud services — but the accounts are tied to email addresses no one has the password for. Years of work, largely irretrievable.

These aren't edge cases. They're becoming common as our financial and creative lives move online.

The annual inventory doesn't take long. But skipping it for three years? That's where the real cost accumulates.

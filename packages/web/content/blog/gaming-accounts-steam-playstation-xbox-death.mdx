## You "Bought" 1,200 Games. Your Family Gets Zero.

You've been gaming since the 90s. Over the years, you've built a Steam library with 1,200 titles. There are blockbusters you picked up at launch, hidden gems from indie bundles, classic games you hunted down. You remember every sale, every wishlist, every late-night purchase.

On Steam's servers, that library is tied to your account. Together, it's worth thousands. Your family knows you loved gaming. They know these games meant something to you.

When you die, they'll get nothing.

Not because they don't care. Not because the law doesn't allow it. But because of a sentence buried in Steam's Subscriber Agreement that you almost certainly never read: your account and everything in it is **personal and non-transferable**. It dies with you.

## The Legal Reality Nobody Talks About

When you buy a game on Steam, PlayStation Store, Xbox, or the Nintendo eShop, you're not buying a game. You're purchasing a **license** — permission to access and play that specific title while your account exists and the platform continues to operate.

This distinction matters enormously when it comes to inheritance.

Physical copies of games can be inherited. A shelf full of PlayStation cartridges is tangible property. Your family can take them, play them, sell them.

But digital games? These are governed by End User License Agreements (EULAs) that all say essentially the same thing:

> **Steam**: "Your Account, including any information pertaining to it... may not be sold, exchanged, transferred, or otherwise conveyed."

> **PlayStation**: "Your account and your wallet funds are personal to you. They may not be transferred."

> **Xbox**: "Your Microsoft account is personal. Content tied to it may not be transferred to another person."

The legal system hasn't caught up. Courts in the UK, US, and most of Europe haven't established clear precedent forcing platforms to honor inheritance claims on digital licenses. Until they do, platforms control the rules — and the rules say your library ends when your account does.

## A Gamer's Family Nightmare

Consider this scenario:

You've been a lifelong gamer. You spent 20 years collecting. Your oldest child grew up watching you play — they know your Steam password and begged to finish your save file on a game you never completed before you got sick.

After you pass, they try to log in. Steam's system flags the login from a new device. The account recovery requires access to the email account — which also needs recovery. By the time it's sorted, months have passed. Steam has no formal process for transferring a deceased user's library. Your child's request is politely declined.

The 1,200 games are gone. The save files. The screenshots. The achievements that took hundreds of hours. Gone.

This isn't hypothetical. Gaming families across Europe and the UK deal with this every year.

## What Each Platform Actually Allows

Let's be honest about what the major platforms offer:

**Steam**: No official inheritance process. Valve's official position is that accounts cannot be transferred. Some families report success in recovering access by providing a death certificate, but this is informal and inconsistent — and still doesn't allow legal transfer to a new owner.

**PlayStation (Sony)**: Sony explicitly states that PSN accounts are non-transferable. Your PlayStation Wallet funds (real money you deposited) cannot be refunded to an estate. Your trophy collection — thousands of hours of proof — is locked.

**Xbox / Microsoft**: Microsoft accounts have a Next of Kin program that allows family members to request access for the purpose of retrieving certain data. But this does not transfer the game library to a new account.

**Nintendo**: Even more restrictive. Nintendo accounts are entirely personal and the platform has no inheritance or estate access process at all.

## Why This Is a Bigger Problem for Expats

If you're living in the Netherlands, Germany, France, or anywhere in Europe outside your home country, this problem compounds.

Estate administration across borders is already complex under EU Succession Regulation (EU 650/2012). When your executor is based in Ireland but your gaming accounts are tied to a UK email address, and the platform's support team is in California, the practical barriers become enormous.

Many expats also build significant gaming libraries over decades, carrying accounts from country to country, accumulating games bought in multiple regional stores. None of that travels with you in any legal sense.

## What You Can Do — Right Now

The good news: you have more options than you think.

**1. Document your accounts**
Write down every platform account, the email linked to it, and the value you place on it. Include this in your digital estate plan. Your executor needs to know these accounts exist.

**2. Use Steam's Family Sharing**
While accounts cannot be inherited, Steam's Family Sharing feature allows you to share your library with up to five family members. If a family member already has access to your shared library before you die, they can continue using it — though this is a gray area, not an official solution.

**3. Xbox Family Groups**
Microsoft's Xbox Family Group allows family members to share game libraries. Setting this up while you're alive means your family has continued access even without account transfer.

**4. Create a shared account strategy**
Some families create a "family account" that multiple members use — not an individual account. This is against most platforms' terms of service, but it sidesteps the inheritance problem.

**5. Prioritize physical copies for games that matter**
For the games that genuinely matter to you — the ones you want a family member to play one day — consider buying physical copies as well as digital. Physical games are property that can be inherited.

**6. Include gaming accounts in your will**
Your will can explicitly name what accounts you want a family member to access and use. While platforms won't recognize this legally, it provides clear intent that can help with informal negotiations and establishes your wishes.

## The Broader Picture

Gaming libraries are just one corner of the larger digital inheritance problem.

Your Spotify playlists. Your Netflix profile. Your cloud photo albums. Your email history. Your social media accounts. All of these live on platforms that don't recognize the legal concept of inheritance. All of them could disappear unless you plan.

The generation that has grown up with digital everything — that has spent thousands building libraries, profiles, and collections — is approaching an age where these questions become urgent.

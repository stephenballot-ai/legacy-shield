## The Collection That Took Years to Build

Think about your Steam library. Go on — open it, count the games. Hundreds of titles. RPGs you binged through late nights, indie gems you found before anyone else, blockbusters you bought on launch day.

Now add up what you paid.

For the average gamer who's been on Steam since 2010, the honest number is somewhere between $2,000 and $8,000. Some collections are worth significantly more. That doesn't include PlayStation Plus, Xbox Game Pass, individual PlayStation Store purchases, or the DLC packs that add up quietly over the years.

When you die, your family inherits none of it.

Not a single game. Not your save files. Not your achievement history. Not the in-game items or skins worth real money. The accounts lock, the licenses terminate, and everything you spent years building simply ceases to exist.

## You Never Owned the Games

This is the part that genuinely shocks most people: you were never buying games. You were buying a license to play them.

Steam's subscriber agreement is explicit:

> "The Content and Services are licensed, not sold. Your license confers no title or ownership in the Content and Services."

Sony's PlayStation Store terms echo this: your purchases grant you a "limited, non-exclusive, non-transferable licence." Microsoft's Xbox terms say the same. The pattern is identical across every major gaming platform.

When you die, that license doesn't pass to your heirs. It terminates. Your surviving partner, your children, your sibling who grew up playing alongside you — they have no legal right to access your account or the library within it.

This isn't a loophole. It's not an oversight. It's how these platforms are deliberately designed.

## What Happens Practically

A family member calls Sony customer support after a death. They want to keep access to the account — maybe to play the games themselves, maybe just to preserve the save files that document the last hundred hours of someone they loved.

The answer, in nearly every case, is no.

Steam has no official account transfer or inheritance process. PlayStation's bereavement policy allows account closure and potentially a refund of unused PlayStation Store credit, but the library itself cannot be transferred. Xbox has a similar position.

There are occasional exceptions — some platforms will, in certain circumstances, allow a verified family member to access an account temporarily. But there is no inheritance process. There is no formal transfer mechanism. The accounts are designed to die with the owner.

## The Real Cost Nobody Talks About

The financial loss is one thing. The emotional loss is something else.

Gaming is one of the most personal digital spaces that exists. Profiles contain years of history — the games someone played when they were grieving, the online friends they made, the characters they built, the worlds they escaped into. For many people, a gaming profile is a genuine record of a life.

Parents have found their late child's Minecraft world and been unable to access it. Partners have wanted to continue a shared save file and been locked out. These aren't edge cases. They happen constantly, and platforms provide no solution.

## The Legal Landscape

A handful of countries have begun addressing digital inheritance. In Germany, the Federal Court of Justice ruled in 2018 that Facebook accounts are inheritable — that the platform must grant heirs access. The logic was clear: digital accounts are part of the estate, and heirs have the same rights to digital assets as physical ones.

But gaming platforms have mounted a different argument. The "licensed content" framework means the digital goods within an account are not legally the user's property. They belong to the platform. The license terminates at death because it was always personal.

This argument has not yet been definitively tested for gaming accounts specifically. In most jurisdictions, it would likely succeed — because you did, after all, agree to those terms when you signed up.

## What You Can Actually Do

The situation isn't hopeless. It requires planning.

**Document everything.** Your heirs can't claim what they don't know exists. Keep a record of your major gaming accounts, account credentials, and a realistic estimate of the value involved. Store this securely — which means not in a document your family can't find.

**Share account access now.** The most effective strategy is family sharing while you're alive. Steam Family Sharing, PlayStation Gameshare, and Xbox Home Xbox features allow you to share your library with a designated account. Set this up now, while you can.

**Understand platform-specific policies.** Microsoft has shown more openness than others to account access after death — explore their bereavement options. Sony and Valve have been less forthcoming.

**Secure your credentials.** If your family can access your account credentials, they can, practically speaking, continue using the account regardless of the terms of service. Many families choose this approach, understanding the technical terms-of-service gray area involved.

**Include gaming accounts in your digital estate plan.** A digital estate plan should catalog all accounts with material value — including your gaming library. The plan should name who gets access and how.

## The Bigger Picture

Gaming is not trivial. The global games market is now larger than the film and music industries combined. Millions of people have invested thousands of hours and thousands of euros into digital collections that the platforms treat as non-existent upon death.

The platforms will not solve this voluntarily. Legislative change is slow. What exists now — the only thing that exists now — is your ability to plan ahead.

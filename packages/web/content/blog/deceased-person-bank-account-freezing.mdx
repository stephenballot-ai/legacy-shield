## The Call Nobody Is Ready For

Your parent dies on a Tuesday. By Thursday, you discover their debit card is declined when you try to pay a utility bill using the account. The bank hasn't been notified yet — and somehow they already know.

This is more common than you'd think. And for families trying to manage immediate expenses after a death, a frozen bank account can feel like a cruel bureaucratic punishment layered on top of grief.

Here is what actually happens, why it happens fast, and what your family can do.

## Why Banks Freeze Accounts After a Death

Banks don't freeze accounts out of malice. They do it for legal reasons — and increasingly, for automated compliance reasons.

When a person dies, the legal authority to operate their accounts transfers away from the deceased. The bank must verify who now has the right to access those funds. Until that verification happens, the safest (and legally required) position is to freeze the account.

There are two main triggers:

**1. Direct notification.** A family member calls the bank or visits a branch to report the death. The bank immediately places a hold on the account.

**2. Automated detection.** Banks in many European countries are connected to civil registration systems. In the Netherlands, the BRP (Basisregistratie Personen) is linked to financial institutions. In Germany, the Melderegister has similar connections. When a death is registered officially, some banks receive automatic alerts — sometimes before the family has even called.

This is why accounts can freeze seemingly on their own.

## What Gets Frozen — And What Doesn't

A death freeze typically applies to:

- Current and savings accounts held solely in the deceased's name
- Investment portfolios held individually
- Credit cards linked to the account

**Joint accounts are different.** If you hold a joint account with the deceased, you generally retain access to your share of the funds. The bank may still flag the account for review, but full freezing of joint accounts is less common — and varies by country and bank.

**Direct debits and standing orders** may continue processing for a short period after the freeze is applied, or may stop immediately. This unpredictability can cause missed mortgage payments or utility disconnections during an already stressful time.

## How Long Does the Freeze Last?

This is the question everyone asks, and the honest answer is: it depends on where you live and how efficiently your family can gather documents.

In **the United Kingdom**, a Grant of Probate (or Letters of Administration if there is no will) is usually required before a bank releases funds. This can take anywhere from 4 to 12 months in practice, though simple estates move faster.

In **the Netherlands**, banks require a *verklaring van erfrecht* (declaration of inheritance) from a notaris. This typically takes 4–8 weeks once the notaris has all the documents.

In **Germany**, the bank needs an *Erbschein* (certificate of inheritance) issued by the Nachlassgericht (probate court), unless a notarized will or inheritance contract is available. Timelines range from 6 weeks to several months.

In **France**, a *certificat d'hérédité* or an *acte de notoriété* drafted by a notaire is required. Simple estates can be resolved in 6 weeks; complex ones can take much longer.

The point is this: **your family will almost certainly face weeks or months without access to these funds.**

## What Your Family Can Do During a Freeze

A frozen account doesn't mean your family is stranded. Here's what can help:

**1. Identify joint accounts or named beneficiaries.** Savings products like life insurance, pension pots, and some savings accounts pass outside of the estate entirely. If you named a beneficiary on your pension or life insurance, that money doesn't go through probate and can be released relatively quickly.

**2. Request a compassionate payment.** Most major banks have a process for releasing limited funds — typically up to a few thousand euros — to cover immediate funeral expenses, even before the estate is fully settled. You need to ask for this explicitly. It won't happen automatically.

**3. Get the paperwork moving immediately.** The single biggest cause of delay is waiting too long to contact a notaris or solicitor. The earlier the process starts, the earlier accounts are unfrozen.

**4. Document everything.** Keep records of every interaction with the bank — dates, names, reference numbers. Banks occasionally lose paperwork, and without your records you may have to restart.

Here's the uncomfortable truth: most people have no idea what accounts their loved ones hold, where the documents are, or even which bank to call.
